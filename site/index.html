The survey · Prologue

“I love…”

More than a hundred people were asked to finish one sentence. No direction, no categories — just the beginning of a thought.

“I love…”

Finishing this sentence turned out to be more difficult than it first seemed.

When more than a hundred respondents were asked to complete it, their answers brought together things that might not usually appear in the same conversation: family, friends, animals, universities, music, food, hobbies, memories, and the ordinary moments that make up a day.

“Only my family,” one person wrote. Another answered simply: “Dancing.” Someone mentioned kittens; someone else, manty, cold Coca-Cola, strawberries, and peonies. There were declarations of love for friends, for oneself, for life itself, and for the feeling of being needed.

One response contained almost an entire personal universe: close people, night walks, Blender, video games, neon signs, and the hope of one day feeling that they had finally found their place.

For one respondent, love fitted into a single word: “Living.” For another, it became a long tribute to a friend, listing her voice, smile, poems, manner of speaking, and even the way she pronounced the letter “r.” Someone wrote about a person living on the other side of the world, whom they still loved very much. Another remembered people who could no longer be brought back.

There was no prescribed direction to the question. Respondents were not asked to think specifically about romance, family, or relationships. They were simply invited to finish a sentence.

The question “I love…” — how would you finish this sentence?

    How to read this survey

    The question was put to more than a hundred respondents. What you see here is a curated set of 74 individual answers, translated into English with their wording, humour and references kept. Some responses quoted in the prologue belong to the wider set and are not among the 74 cards. The answers show the range of ways people describe love; they are not a statistically representative portrait of any wider population, and the themes are editorial groupings rather than measurements.

    We are used to thinking of love as a story between two people. Cinema often gives it a familiar structure: two people meet, fall in love, encounter obstacles, and eventually reach a happy or tragic ending. But the answers to one simple question suggest that love is much less confined than that.

    It can be romantic, familial, friendly, or directed towards an animal. It can be an attitude towards oneself or a feeling connected to the world around us.

    To understand what these different forms might look like in real life, I spoke with four people. Their stories do not create one universal definition. Instead, each reveals a different part of what it means to care, to become attached, and to feel connected.

    A Romantic love

    Darina and Adil

    Love that is tested by time

    • Darina, 19
    • Adil, 21
    • The same school
    • Almost two years before becoming a couple

    In romantic films, events often move quickly. Two people meet, something happens between them, obstacles appear, and the audience waits to discover how the story will end.

    In Shakespeare’s Romeo and Juliet, love seems to ignite almost immediately, unfolding against the hostility between two families. In Jane Austen’s Pride and Prejudice, the process is slower. The characters must reconsider their first impressions, recognise their mistakes, and learn to understand one another.

    Darina and Adil’s relationship is closer to the second kind of story. It did not begin with a sudden confession or an immediate certainty. Before they became a couple, almost two years passed.

    Darina is nineteen. Adil is twenty-one. They went to the same school. In the ninth grade, Adil once sent Darina a message, but she did not even realise who he was. Later, they followed and unfollowed each other on social media, occasionally exchanging short messages: “Hi,” “Bye,” “How are you?” Nothing that either of them would have called the beginning of a relationship at the time.

    The turning point came on Adil’s graduation day. Darina approached the graduates with her classmates to take photographs. They wished one another good luck and went their separate ways.

    That evening, Adil replied to one of her social media stories.

    They began talking more often. Eventually, they started going for walks almost every day.

    Their first impressions of one another were not entirely accurate. Darina had expected Adil to behave differently. On their first walk, however, they simply sat on a bench, hugged, and went for a walk. He offered to buy her something to eat or drink and paid for it himself. Darina remembers being surprised because she had expected to take out her own money.

    Adil, meanwhile, had imagined Darina as more outgoing and sociable than she turned out to be in person.

    As they spent more time together, Darina became increasingly interested in him. She began writing first more often and suggesting that they meet. Adil also showed initiative. He walked her home, and once, shortly before he admitted his feelings, he hugged her, kissed her on the forehead, and ran away.

    In the end, Darina was the first to say how she felt.

    One of the moments she remembers most clearly did not happen during a date or a grand romantic gesture. It happened on her birthday.

    On 9 January 2024, Adil came to Darina’s home with a present. He was the only guest. Her parents had prepared a table and asked him to make a toast.

    Adil wished her success and promised to support her in whatever she wanted to achieve.

    Darina began to cry.

    She remembers that evening as the moment when she understood particularly clearly how important Adil had become to her. His words did not remain a beautiful promise made for one night. According to Darina, he continued to help her and support her ambitions in the years that followed.

    Their relationship has not always been easy. Darina says that the first year involved misunderstandings and arguments. After one particularly difficult conflict, she travelled to Almaty. They decided to take a short break to think through their feelings.

    Even while they were apart, however, they continued communicating and worrying about one another.

    For Darina, this became one of the signs of closeness: a person can make you angry, hurt your feelings, or disappoint you, and you can still care about what happens to them.

    The couple also have a small ritual that Darina considers almost essential. From the beginning of their communication, they have wished each other good morning and good night. In more than three years, she says, there have been only a few days when they did not do it.

    For her, these messages are not an empty formality. Waking up and finding out how Adil is doing makes her feel that the day has started properly.

    What she values most about him is his calmness.

    Darina describes herself as an anxious person. She can worry about different situations and sometimes panic. Adil, by contrast, tries to approach problems rationally. He listens, helps her understand what is happening, and sometimes simply tells her that everything will be all right.

    Over time, the relationship has also changed Darina’s understanding of how people should treat each other.

    She remembers that, in her earlier surroundings, rude words and sharp ways of addressing a partner were sometimes treated as normal. Something similar happened between her and Adil once. He explained that he did not like being spoken to that way.

    They discussed it, and Darina decided not to cross that boundary again.

    Now she believes that even during an argument, partners should not humiliate each other. They should explain their positions and make an effort to listen.

    “Hearing and listening are different things,” she says.

    When I asked Darina why she loves Adil, she spoke about how he thinks of her every day, believes in her, helps her, and tries to do the things she asks of him.

    Then she added a sentence that seemed to capture her understanding of their relationship:

    “I choose him every time. And he chooses me every day.”

    — Darina, 19

    Their story is not built around one dramatic declaration. It is made up of small decisions repeated over time: writing first, walking someone home, remembering an important day, listening after an argument, and showing up when support is needed.

    Perhaps this is one reason why their relationship feels closer to Austen than to Shakespeare. Its meaning is not contained in a single moment of passion. It develops through the gradual discovery of another person.

    B Self-love

    Aruzhan

    Learning to stay on your own side

    • 18 years old
    • Second year of university
    • Demanding of herself

    In Greta Gerwig’s Barbie, the main character begins to question who she is beyond the image she has been expected to embody. Behind the bright colours and humour, the film raises a serious question: can a person accept themselves when they do not fit an idea of perfection?

    For Aruzhan, the answer did not arrive through one dramatic event.

    She is eighteen and in her second year of university. She says that she has generally had a good relationship with herself. With age, however, that relationship has become more conscious.

    For her, self-love does not mean believing that she is better than everyone else. It does not mean convincing herself that she is perfect.

    It means understanding that her value does not disappear when something goes wrong.

    Aruzhan is demanding of herself, especially when it comes to studying and personal achievements. She wants to do things well, sometimes perfectly. When the result does not match her expectations, she can become upset.

    Over time, however, she has learned to separate the evaluation of an action from the evaluation of herself as a person.

    She can be disappointed in a mistake without deciding that she is a failure.

    “My mistakes and failures do not reduce my value as a person.”

    — Aruzhan, 18

    In Aruzhan’s understanding, self-love does not mean giving up on personal development. She continues to set goals and work on herself, but tries not to turn every unsuccessful attempt into a reason for self-criticism.

    She also wants to leave room for things that have nothing to do with achievement: spending time with friends, listening to music, reading, or simply being alone.

    Personal boundaries are another important part of this understanding.

    Aruzhan believes that caring about other people matters, but it should not become a habit of constantly pushing one's own needs aside. If something makes her uncomfortable, she tries to say so directly.

    Refusing someone is not always easy, she admits. Still, the right to say no does not make a person selfish.

    It is difficult to talk about self-perception today without mentioning social media.

    Aruzhan believes that social platforms can influence self-esteem. Every day, people see other people's achievements, carefully chosen photographs, and images that make it seem as though everyone else is doing well.

    She tries to remember that these posts show only a part of someone's life. Comparing oneself with that selection is rarely useful. Paying attention to one's own progress is more meaningful.

    Aruzhan does not claim to feel confident all the time. She also has difficult days, moments of exhaustion, and doubts.

    For her, self-love is precisely about not turning away from herself during those moments.

    Her position is not simply the familiar advice that a person must love themselves before they can love anyone else. Aruzhan does not believe that someone must complete a particular journey towards self-acceptance before being able to care for another person.

    She is talking about something more practical: respecting one's own feelings can make it easier to recognise and respect the feelings of others.

    When I asked what she loves about herself, Aruzhan answered simply:

    “I love myself simply because I am me.”

    — Aruzhan

    Not because of achievements. Not because she never makes mistakes. Not because she always meets someone else's expectations.

    Because she does not have to be perfect to deserve love.

    Self-love is often presented as a confident statement, as if it were something a person either possesses or lacks. Aruzhan's account suggests something quieter: it can be a way of responding to yourself when life does not go according to plan.

    It is not the absence of criticism. It is the ability to disagree with your own actions without rejecting yourself entirely.

    C Love for an animal

    Rory

    When caring means letting go

    • Rory is the cat
    • Found two years ago
    • The person who found her is not named

    In literature and cinema, animals often become the centre of stories about loyalty and devotion.

    In Gavriil Troepolsky’s White Bim Black Ear, the relationship between a human and a dog is explored through trust, dependence, and responsibility. Such stories can make it easy to focus on the feelings of the human character.

    But there is another side to the relationship.

    An animal is not simply a symbol of affection or a way to make a human life warmer. It is a living being with its own needs, fears, and ways of responding to the world.

    Rory’s story makes this distinction particularly clear.

    Two years ago, the person I interviewed was returning home in cold, rainy weather. At the time, they had not yet moved to Astana to study.

    On the street, they noticed a cat. There were no other cats nearby, and the animal appeared to be alone. The person felt sorry for leaving it outside in the cold and decided to take it home.

    They understood that their parents might not approve.

    For the first couple of days, the cat was kept on the balcony. Eventually, the family found out about the animal. When the parents asked where it had come from, the person explained honestly that they had found it outside.

    The cat was named Rory. The name, the interviewee remembers, simply came to mind.

    They speak about Rory warmly. She was cute and funny, and they enjoyed spending time with her and playing with her.

    But there was another person in the household whose needs also had to be considered: a younger brother who was about four years old at the time.

    A small child does not always know how to handle an animal gently. After some time, Rory became frightened. She began to fear people in the house and hide from them.

    Spending time with her became increasingly difficult.

    Eventually, the family decided to give Rory to other people, hoping that she would feel more comfortable in a different home.

    This story does not have the familiar happy ending in which an animal stays with the person who found it forever.

    But giving Rory another home does not necessarily mean that the person stopped caring about her.

    Sometimes care means recognising that an animal is uncomfortable in its current environment and that another place may be better for it.

    In their 2022 review, The Complexity of the Human–Animal Bond, researchers Emanuela Prato-Previde, Elisa Basso Ricci, and Elisa Silvia Colombo examine human–animal relationships through empathy, attachment, and anthropomorphism.[2]

    They describe these relationships as complex and potentially beneficial for both humans and animals. At the same time, they warn that human–animal relationships can also have negative consequences when the animal's needs and welfare are overlooked.

    This distinction matters.

    A person's emotional attachment does not automatically mean that an animal is doing well. Caring for an animal involves more than enjoying its presence or wanting to keep it close. It also means paying attention to its behaviour, recognising signs of distress, and being willing to change the situation when necessary.

    Rory did not remain with the person who found her. But her story became part of that person's life.

    They remember finding her on a cold, rainy day, giving her a name, and spending time with her.

    Perhaps one of the more difficult aspects of loving an animal is accepting that affection does not always mean keeping it beside you at any cost.

    Sometimes, responsibility asks for a different kind of decision.

    Magzhan and Go Roflit

    A friendship where you can be yourself

    • Magzhan, 19
    • Friends for about a year
    • Met at ITO Music Club

    How the group became close

    From a performance to Go Roflit

    Films about friendship often contain a moment when two characters stop being merely familiar with one another.

    In The Intouchables, this happens through the everyday life shared by two people who initially seem very different. In Friends, closeness is built through recurring meetings, shared jokes, and the habit of being part of one another's ordinary lives.

    Real friendships rarely begin with a solemn promise.

    More often, a person gradually notices that there are people around whom they do not have to choose every word carefully or explain every reaction.

    For nineteen-year-old Magzhan, that group is Go Roflit.

    They have known each other for about a year. They first met at a performance at ITO Music Club. At first, they were not particularly close. Gradually, however, they became more comfortable with one another and developed into a group.

    The first thing Magzhan noticed about his new acquaintances was their talent. He describes them as people who are good not only at music but also at other things.

    But talent was not the reason they became important to him.

    It was the sense of freedom he felt around them.

    He can talk to his friends about almost anything without worrying that they will judge him. He especially values the fact that they try to understand one another before reacting with anger or aggression.

    The group expresses care in different ways: through gifts, attention, support, and jokes that only make sense to the people involved.

    Interactive · five ways

    How friendship is expressed in Go Roflit

    One of Magzhan's most memorable experiences happened during a rehearsal at the music club.

    One of the group members had travelled from far away, in freezing weather. By the time he arrived, they discovered that the rehearsal had been cancelled.

    In the end, the friends simply hugged him and spent time together.

    Nothing particularly extraordinary happened. But the episode stayed with Magzhan because someone had made a long journey, and the others did not leave him alone when the original plan fell through.

    He remembers another experience more carefully.

    After the end of a previous relationship, Magzhan went through a difficult period. For a long time, he did not tell his friends what was happening. He kept everything to himself until one day he broke down in front of one of them.

    His friend did not demand an explanation. He simply hugged him and listened.

    Magzhan emphasises that this feeling of support does not come from just one person. He knows that he can turn to the group when he is having a difficult time.

    They will not judge him or demand that he explain every detail immediately.

    Of course, friendships also have conflicts.

    Magzhan admits that they have hurt one another's feelings, sometimes quite seriously. But they continue to communicate because they try to understand what happened rather than assume that the other person intended to cause harm.

    In his 2025 review, Why Friendship and Loneliness Affect Our Health, Robin I. M. Dunbar examines friendship as an important part of human social life, rather than something secondary to family or romantic relationships.[3]

    He discusses the relationship between close social connections, emotional support, loneliness, and wellbeing. He also emphasises that friendships require time and effort. They are not maintained simply because two people once became close.

    This is an important point.

    Friendship is not only about having people to spend time with. It is also about maintaining a relationship through ordinary contact, shared experiences, difficult conversations, and the willingness to show up for one another.

    Magzhan's story illustrates what this can look like in everyday life.

    Sometimes support is a long conversation. Sometimes it is a hug after a wasted journey to a cancelled rehearsal. Sometimes it is simply having somewhere to go when pretending that everything is fine becomes impossible.

    When I asked Magzhan why he loves his friends, he thought for a moment and then answered:

    “Well, because I love them. Do I need any other reason?”

    — Magzhan, 19

    The biology of love

    What biology can explain — and what it cannot

    Love is an emotion. But it is also a process involving the brain, the body, motivation, memory, and social connection.

    Love is often described as something impossible to explain.

    It can feel sudden, irrational, overwhelming, or difficult to put into words. Yet scientists have been studying the processes involved in attraction, attachment, and social bonding for decades.

    Biology cannot tell us exactly why one person falls in love with another, why a particular friendship becomes important, or why someone remembers a small gesture years later.

    But it can help explain some of the processes that make human connection possible.

    Love is not controlled by one chemical or one isolated area of the brain. It involves a network of biological systems associated with motivation, reward, emotion, memory, and social behaviour.

    A 2025 review by Jaroslava Babková and Gabriela Repiská, The Molecular Basis of Love, examines the roles of several hormones and neuromodulators, including dopamine, oxytocin, vasopressin, and serotonin.[1]

    Their review offers a useful starting point for understanding why love is not simply an abstract feeling. It is also connected to the way the brain and body respond to other people.

    Before we start

    There is no single “love chemical”.

    Different systems take part in attraction, motivation, attachment and social behaviour. They overlap and interact, and none of them creates love on its own.

    Neurotransmitter
    A chemical messenger passed between nerve cells.
    Hormone
    A messenger carried in the blood to act elsewhere in the body.
    Neuromodulator
    A messenger that adjusts how whole groups of neurons respond, rather than passing one signal.

    Dopamine: the motivation to move closer

    Dopamine is involved in the brain's reward and motivation systems.

    It is often described in popular culture as the “happiness chemical,” but this explanation is incomplete. Dopamine is not simply a substance that produces pleasure. It is also involved in motivation, learning, anticipation, and the reinforcement of behaviour.

    In the context of attraction, these processes can help explain why a person becomes particularly attentive to someone they like.

    A message can become something to look forward to. A planned meeting can occupy someone's thoughts throughout the day. Small interactions can become meaningful because the brain learns to associate them with rewarding experiences.

    This does not mean that dopamine is love itself.

    Rather, it is one part of the biological machinery involved in wanting, seeking, and repeating experiences that matter to us.

    Oxytocin: connection and social bonding

    Oxytocin is another molecule frequently mentioned in discussions of love. It is often called the “love hormone.”

    The nickname is memorable, but it can be misleading.

    Oxytocin is involved in a range of physiological and social processes. Researchers study it in relation to social interaction, attachment, caregiving, and bonding.

    The scientific picture is more complicated than the popular idea that oxytocin automatically makes people trust one another or fall in love.

    Babková and Repiská discuss research examining oxytocin concentrations alongside people's experiences of romantic relationships.

    One study involving 129 adults found an association between higher oxytocin concentrations and more positive perceptions of romantic partners.

    Another study involving 34 young married couples found that higher oxytocin levels were associated with lower relationship distress and higher reported relationship quality.

    Reading the cards

    Oxytocin ↔ How the relationship feels

    What these studies measured: two things that tend to appear together.

    Oxytocin → Love

    What they did not show: that one causes the other.

    These findings are interesting, but they need to be interpreted carefully.

    An association does not prove that oxytocin causes a person to love their partner. It does not show that a hormone level can predict whether a relationship will last or whether two people are compatible.

    Human relationships are shaped by many factors, including individual experience, communication, environment, expectations, and social circumstances.

    Oxytocin is one piece of a much larger picture.

    Vasopressin: social behaviour and attachment

    Vasopressin is another hormone studied in relation to social behaviour and pair bonding.

    Research on vasopressin has included studies of animal bonding systems, which have helped scientists investigate how biological mechanisms may contribute to long-term social attachment.

    However, findings from animal research cannot automatically be transferred to humans.

    Human relationships are influenced by language, culture, personal history, social expectations, and individual choice. Biology provides part of the explanation, not a complete account.

    From voles to people

    In prairie voles, differences in vasopressin receptors in specific brain regions have been linked to fidelity and pair bonding. Yet a 2023 study found that voles without a working oxytocin receptor still formed social bonds — a reminder that these systems are more complicated than “one molecule, one behaviour”.[1a]

    Serotonin: a more complicated relationship with emotion

    Serotonin is involved in several processes, including mood regulation and other aspects of brain function.

    It is sometimes mentioned in discussions of romantic attraction, but the relationship between serotonin and love is not simple enough to be reduced to a single explanation.

    It would be misleading to suggest that a particular serotonin level determines whether someone is in love.

    The same caution applies to all attempts to explain complex emotions through one biological measurement.

    What the evidence is — and is not

    A 1999 study measured serotonin transporters on blood platelets and found lower values in people in the early stage of romantic love, similar to values in people with obsessive–compulsive disorder. Blood is not the brain: the review concludes that whether serotonin in the brain is genuinely lower “remains unresolved”.[1a]

    Attraction, attachment, and long-term bonding

    It is also useful to distinguish between attraction, romantic motivation, emotional attachment, and long-term bonding.

    These experiences overlap, but they are not identical.

    Attraction can make someone particularly interested in another person. Motivation can encourage them to seek contact. Attachment involves a sense of emotional connection and significance. Long-term bonding develops through a more sustained relationship.

    These are not necessarily fixed stages that every couple experiences in the same order.

    Different relationships develop differently. Some begin with friendship. Others involve immediate attraction. Some grow gradually, while others change over time.

    ✓ What researchers can say

    • Several messengers — dopamine, oxytocin, vasopressin, serotonin and others — are involved in attraction, motivation and social bonding.
    • Reward and motivation systems are active in people who report being intensely in love.
    • Some hormone levels are associated with how people perceive their partners and relationships.
    • Animal models, such as prairie voles, help show how bonding mechanisms can work.

    ? What remains uncertain

    • Whether these associations reflect causes, effects, or something else entirely.
    • How far animal findings apply to human relationships.
    • Whether serotonin in the brain is genuinely lower in early love.
    • How culture shapes biological responses — the review notes that data here are very limited.
    • Why this person, this friendship, this memory.

    Darina and Adil's story, for example, is not defined by one sudden moment. Their relationship developed through repeated interactions, shared experiences, and everyday support.

    Magzhan's friendship also cannot be explained by one biological mechanism. It involves trust built over time, a sense of belonging, and the experience of being accepted by other people.

    Biology can help us understand why human connection matters and how some of its underlying processes work.

    It cannot explain the whole meaning of a particular relationship.

    A molecule cannot tell us why a birthday toast made Darina cry. It cannot describe what Magzhan felt when his friend hugged him without asking questions. It cannot determine why someone remembers a cat they found in the rain.

    Those meanings belong to the stories people tell about their lives.

    04

    The psychology of love

    The psychology of connection

    Concepts, not diagnoses. Nothing on this page can tell you your “type” — and it does not try to.

    Biology helps explain some of the mechanisms involved in human connection. Psychology asks different questions.

    How do people learn to trust one another? Why do some relationships make us feel secure? How do we understand our own needs while remaining attentive to someone else's?

    There is no single psychological explanation for love, either. But several concepts can help us understand the experiences described in the four interviews.

    Six expandable concept cards

    A small vocabulary for the four stories

    Definition. The emotional bonds people form with significant others — first studied in childhood, now also in adult friendships and partnerships.

    In the stories. Darina keeps caring what happens to Adil even during a break.

    Research can tell usPeople differ in how they respond to closeness, distance and uncertainty.

    It cannot tell usYour “style” from a quiz, or what a particular relationship means.

    02Emotional security→ Darina and Adil

    Definition. The sense that problems can be raised and discussed without the relationship itself being at risk.

    In the stories. Adil’s calm and his willingness to listen when Darina becomes anxious.

    Research can tell usFeeling safe with someone shapes how people handle conflict and stress.

    It cannot tell usThat a secure relationship has no disagreements — it does.

    03Self-compassion→ Aruzhan

    Definition. Responding to your own difficulties and mistakes with understanding rather than constant punishment.

    In the stories. Aruzhan can be disappointed in a result without deciding she is a failure.

    Research can tell usHow people treat themselves after failure is distinct from how highly they rate themselves.

    It cannot tell usThat self-compassion means lowering your standards.

    It cannot tell usHow many friends you should have.

    Attachment and emotional security

    Attachment describes the emotional bonds people form with significant others.

    Attachment theory is often discussed in relation to childhood, but researchers also use it to examine close relationships throughout adult life, including romantic partnerships and friendships.

    People can differ in how they respond to closeness, distance, uncertainty, and emotional support.

    Some feel relatively comfortable depending on others and allowing others to depend on them. Others may find closeness difficult or feel particularly anxious when a relationship becomes uncertain.

    These patterns are sometimes discussed through attachment styles. They can be useful frameworks, but they should not be treated as fixed personality labels or clinical diagnoses.

    Darina's account offers an everyday example of why emotional security can matter.

    She values Adil's calmness and his willingness to listen when she becomes anxious. His support does not remove every difficulty from their relationship, but it helps her feel that problems can be discussed rather than faced alone.

    Security is not the absence of disagreement. It is the sense that disagreement does not automatically mean the relationship is over.

    Self-worth and self-compassion

    Self-love is another phrase that has become common in popular culture.

    It can refer to several related but different ideas:

    01

    Self-esteem

    How a person evaluates their own worth.

    Not: believing you are better than everyone else.

    02

    Self-acceptance

    Recognising oneself without requiring perfection.

    Not: deciding you are already perfect.

    03

    Self-compassion

    Responding to personal difficulties with understanding rather than constant punishment.

    Not: never feeling disappointed.

    04

    Personal boundaries

    Recognising and communicating what feels acceptable or unacceptable.

    Not: selfishness, or needing nobody.

    05

    Personal development

    Making changes without treating every mistake as proof of personal failure.

    Not: a test you must pass before you deserve kindness.

    These ideas do not require a person to be confident all the time.

    Aruzhan's account reflects this distinction. She still wants to achieve her goals and can become disappointed when things do not go as planned. But she tries not to turn one unsuccessful result into a judgement about her entire value as a person.

    Self-respect does not mean refusing to change. It means being able to change without treating yourself as someone who deserves no kindness.

    Self-love also does not mean believing one is perfect, or never needing other people.

    Friendship, belonging, and loneliness

    Friendship is sometimes treated as a secondary form of love, especially in stories that focus on romance.

    But close friendships can provide emotional support, a sense of belonging, and continuity through different periods of life.

    Robin Dunbar's 2025 review, Why Friendship and Loneliness Affect Our Health, examines the relationship between close social connections and wellbeing.[3]

    The review discusses how the number and quality of close relationships are associated with mental and physical health. It also explores the role of social networks and the biological processes involved in maintaining social bonds.

    Dunbar discusses different layers of social connection, including a small inner circle of particularly close relationships. His work describes approximately five close friends as one typical layer in this model.

    This should not be understood as a universal target. People have different social needs, circumstances, and relationship structures. The number is part of a research model, not a rule that everyone must follow.

    One of the review's important points is that friendships require investment.

    They need time, attention, and repeated contact. A friendship does not necessarily remain close simply because it was once important.

    Magzhan's story reflects this ordinary work of maintaining a relationship. His friends support one another through shared activities, difficult conversations, arguments, and simple moments spent together.

    The cancelled rehearsal became memorable not because anything extraordinary happened, but because the group chose to stay together after the original plan failed.

    Sometimes belonging is built precisely through such ordinary moments.

    Human–animal attachment and responsibility

    The psychological experience of attachment is not limited to relationships between humans.

    People can form strong emotional bonds with companion animals. These relationships may involve affection, comfort, routine, and a sense of companionship.

    The review by Prato-Previde, Basso Ricci, and Colombo examines the human–animal bond through empathy, attachment, and anthropomorphism — the tendency to attribute human characteristics or emotions to animals.

    The authors describe human–animal relationships as complex. They can be beneficial for both parties, but they can also become harmful if the animal's welfare is neglected.

    This is particularly relevant to Rory's story.

    The person who found her wanted to help and enjoyed having her at home. But the household environment eventually became difficult for the cat.

    The emotional bond mattered, but so did Rory's experience of living in that environment.

    Responsible care requires more than good intentions. It requires paying attention to the needs of the other living being.

    Across these different forms of connection, one idea keeps returning: love is not only about what a person feels. It is also about how they respond to someone else's needs.

    05

    Love in literature and cinema

    How stories have taught us to imagine love

    An exhibition of six works — a play, two novels, two films and a television series — each placed beside the documentary story it speaks to.

    Long before researchers began studying the biological processes associated with attraction and attachment, writers and filmmakers were already trying to describe what it means to love.

    Their answers have not always been realistic, and they have not always agreed with one another.

    Some stories present love as an overwhelming force. Others show it as a gradual process of understanding. Some focus on romance, while others explore friendship, identity, or the connection between humans and animals.

    Literature and cinema do not provide scientific explanations. They offer cultural images through which people recognise, question, and interpret their own experiences.

    I

    William Shakespeare · play

    Story A · Darina and Adil

    Romeo and Juliet: love against the world

    William Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet remains one of the best-known stories of romantic love.

    The two young characters fall in love in a world shaped by hostility between their families. Their relationship develops quickly, and the conflict surrounding them becomes inseparable from the tragedy of the play.

    The story has influenced how generations imagine passionate love: intense, absolute, and powerful enough to challenge the world around it.

    But it also raises a question about the relationship between love and circumstance.

    How much can two people control when their relationship exists within a larger social conflict?

    Darina and Adil's story is very different in scale and circumstance. Yet both stories invite us to think about the pressures that can surround a relationship.

    The difference is that Darina and Adil's account is not defined by tragedy. Its central idea is the gradual choice to remain attentive to one another.

    II

    Jane Austen · novel

    Story A · Darina and Adil

    Pride and Prejudice: learning to see another person

    Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice offers a different model of romantic development.

    Elizabeth Bennet and Mr Darcy begin with mistaken impressions of one another. Their relationship changes as they reconsider their assumptions, recognise their own flaws, and learn to see beyond appearances.

    The story suggests that understanding another person is not always immediate.

    Sometimes it requires admitting that the first interpretation was wrong.

    This is one reason why Darina and Adil's story feels closer to Austen's gradual approach than to the sudden intensity of Romeo and Juliet.

    Their relationship developed through repeated encounters. They did not know each other fully at the beginning. Their understanding changed as they spent more time together.

    III

    Greta Gerwig · film, 2023

    Story B · Aruzhan

    Barbie: identity beyond expectations

    Greta Gerwig's Barbie uses a bright, carefully constructed fictional world to explore identity, expectations, and self-perception.

    The film asks what happens when someone begins to question the role they have been expected to perform.

    Its relevance to Aruzhan's story lies not in a direct comparison between the characters, but in the shared question of self-acceptance.

    What remains of our sense of worth when we stop measuring ourselves against an ideal?

    Aruzhan's answer is quieter than the film's visual world. She does not claim to have reached a state of perfect confidence. Instead, she tries to remember that mistakes do not erase her value.

    IV

    Gavriil Troepolsky · novel, 1971

    Story C · Rory

    White Bim Black Ear: the responsibility behind affection

    Gavriil Troepolsky's White Bim Black Ear is a story about the bond between a human and a dog.

    Its emotional force comes from the relationship between affection, trust, dependence, and vulnerability.

    Stories about animals can be particularly powerful because they remind readers that the animal cannot always explain what it needs in words.

    The human character must learn to notice.

    This is the question at the centre of Rory's story, too. Affection alone does not guarantee that an animal feels safe. The person responsible for an animal must be attentive to its experience, not only to their own feelings.

    V

    Nakache & Toledano · film, 2011

    Story D · Magzhan

    The Intouchables: friendship across difference

    In The Intouchables (also known as 1+1), friendship develops between two people whose lives and circumstances are very different.

    The film explores how connection can emerge through shared time, humour, trust, and mutual recognition.

    It offers a reminder that friendship does not always begin with similarity. Sometimes it develops because two people discover that they can be themselves around one another.

    This idea connects to Magzhan's description of Go Roflit. He values his friends not only for their abilities, but because he feels free to speak openly and does not expect to be judged.

    VI

    Crane & Kauffman · TV, 1994–2004

    Story D · Magzhan

    Friends: love in ordinary repetitions

    The television series Friends presents friendship through recurring meetings, shared jokes, everyday rituals, and the accumulation of common memories.

    Its world is stylised and often comedic, but its central idea is recognisable: closeness is created through repeated presence.

    A friendship is not only the sum of its dramatic moments. It is also made of conversations, habits, familiar places, and the knowledge that someone will probably be there next time.

    This is close to what Magzhan describes.

    The group does not need to turn every meeting into a major event. Sometimes simply spending time together is enough.

    Taken together, these works show that culture has never offered just one image of love.

    Love can be a tragedy, a process of reconsideration, a question of identity, an act of responsibility, or the ordinary rhythm of friendship.

    The stories we watch and read do not tell us exactly how to live. But they give us language and images with which to think about our own relationships.

    06

    Love and everyday life in the digital age

    How we talk about love today

    Social media has changed the way people talk about relationships.

    A feed can contain romantic declarations, photographs of couples, family celebrations, groups of friends, and videos of pets. Love has become part of everyday digital communication: something people share, discuss, display, and sometimes evaluate.

    Online, it is easy to encounter lists of “green flags” and “red flags,” discussions of love languages, and advice about what an ideal partner should do.

    These ideas can be useful. They give people vocabulary for describing their needs, recognising uncomfortable behaviour, and discussing what they expect from a relationship.

    But they can become restrictive when treated as universal instructions.

    A relationship cannot always be judged by a checklist. People have different ways of expressing affection, different expectations, and different circumstances.

    Three popular concepts

    Useful as a vocabulary — restrictive as a rulebook

    “Green flags”

    Useful whenit helps name what feels safe: listening, keeping promises, respecting a “no”.

    Restrictive whena person is graded against someone else’s list instead of being known.

    “Red flags”

    Useful whenit helps recognise uncomfortable or harmful behaviour — like humiliation during an argument.

    Restrictive whenevery disagreement becomes proof that the relationship is over.

    “Love languages”

    Useful whenit reminds us that care can look different — words, time, gifts, help, touch.

    Restrictive whena popular self-help idea is treated as a scientific test of compatibility.

    The same is true of self-perception.

    Aruzhan points to another side of social media: the constant possibility of comparing oneself with other people's lives.

    People publish achievements, attractive photographs, celebrations, and successful moments. What remains outside the frame is often less visible: uncertainty, exhaustion, arguments, and disappointment.

    It is easy to compare an ordinary day in one's own life with a carefully selected moment from someone else's.

    Aruzhan tries to remember that a social media post represents only part of a person's experience.

    Her observation also connects to the way Darina describes her relationship with Adil.

    For her, the important things are not necessarily public gestures. They are the good-morning messages, the support during difficult periods, and the ability to talk after an argument.

    Magzhan's account is similarly grounded in experiences that do not require an audience. He talks about being able to go to his friends and cry without fearing that they will judge him.

    Social media can be a place where people express affection and find support. It can help people maintain relationships across distance and share moments that matter.

    But a public declaration of love and the experience of closeness are not the same thing.

    Some of the most important moments in a relationship happen without an audience.

    They may never become photographs or posts. They may remain known only to the people who were there.

    That does not make them less meaningful.

    07

    Synthesis

    Four stories, four forms of love

    The four interviews do not describe one universal experience.

    Darina and Adil talk about romantic love as a process of choosing one another, communicating, and offering support over time.

    Aruzhan describes self-love as the ability to recognise her own value without demanding perfection from herself.

    Rory's story shows that affection for an animal can involve difficult decisions, especially when care and the animal's wellbeing do not point in the same direction.

    Magzhan describes friendship as a space where he can be honest, vulnerable, and accepted.

    A navigation aid, not a substitute for the stories.
    StoryForm of loveCentral idea
    Darina and AdilRomantic loveChoosing one another through time and difficulty
    AruzhanSelf-loveAcceptance, care, and personal value
    RoryLove for an animalAffection, responsibility, and letting go
    MagzhanFriendshipTrust, belonging, and emotional support

    These forms of love are different, but they share certain questions.

    • How do we recognise another person's needs?
    • How do we communicate when something goes wrong?
    • How do we remain attentive without losing sight of ourselves?
    • How do we know when care means staying, changing, or letting go?

    There are no universal answers.

    A romantic relationship, a friendship, the bond with an animal, and a person's relationship with themselves cannot be measured by the same standards.

    But each asks for some form of attention.

    Darina and Adil's story is full of everyday choices. Aruzhan's is about maintaining a relationship with herself even when she is disappointed. Rory's story reminds us that another living being's comfort matters. Magzhan's friendship shows how trust can grow through repeated acts of presence.

    Perhaps the common element is not one particular emotion or biological process.

    It is the willingness to recognise that something or someone matters — and to act accordingly.

    08

    Epilogue

    Everyone has their own continuation

    At the beginning of this story was a question:

    “I love…”

    The answers brought together families, friends, romantic partners, pets, music, food, dancing, universities, hobbies, and life itself.

    Some people responded with one word. Others wrote long declarations in which every detail seemed worth preserving.

    The four interviews showed how different the relationships behind those answers can be.

    Darina and Adil are learning to choose each other not only on happy days, but also after disagreements.

    Aruzhan reminds us that accepting ourselves does not require perfection and does not mean giving up on personal growth.

    Rory's story shows that care can sometimes mean recognising that another living being may be better off somewhere else.

    Magzhan speaks about friendship as a place where people can express their emotions without searching for the perfect words.

    There is no single recipe connecting these experiences.

    And perhaps there does not need to be one.

    Love does not have to look the same to be real. For one person, it may begin with a confession. For another, it may mean learning to say no. It may be the decision to take a cold, frightened cat off the street. It may be a friend's embrace when there is nothing useful to say.

    Biology can describe some of the processes involved in attraction and attachment. Psychology can help us understand emotional security, self-worth, and social connection. Literature and cinema can offer images through which we interpret our experiences.

    But none of these perspectives can fully replace the individual meaning of a relationship.

    That meaning is found in the details: the message sent before going to sleep, the promise made at a birthday table, the decision to listen, the memory of a rainy afternoon, or the knowledge that there are people to turn to when life becomes difficult.

    Perhaps this is why the sentence “I love…” is so difficult to finish with one answer.

    It can continue in dozens of different ways.

    And each time, it can become someone's real story.
